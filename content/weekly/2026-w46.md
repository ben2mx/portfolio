**November 9 to 15, 2026**

## What shipped

- **Took a hidden CPU load off a switch stack that serves our headquarters desks.** The two desk-floor switch stacks ran their processors at about 70% all day, with short spikes to 99%. At that level a switch can drop packets, and users were seeing exactly that. About half the load came from device tracking, a feature that keeps a list of every device the switch sees. Nobody had configured it: turning on DHCP snooping (the guard against rogue DHCP servers) switches it on quietly in the background.
- **Found why the list was so big.** One stack was tracking about 4,900 devices, but only about 25 had picked up an address through it. The rest came in over the uplink to the core switch. Address lookups (ARP broadcasts) from the whole building reach that link, so the switch was keeping track of the entire building, not just its own desk ports.
- **The fix was one setting.** I marked the uplink as a trusted port leading to another switch, so device tracking ignores what arrives there and only tracks devices plugged into its own ports. DHCP snooping keeps guarding against rogue DHCP servers. No VLAN, routing or user-facing change. Result on the first stack: processor load fell from 68% to 10%, device tracking's share fell from about half to under 1%, and the list shrank from 4,899 devices to 31. The uplink stayed up the whole time with no dropped pings.
- **Ran it as a script with its own brakes.** The change rides over the same uplink it modifies, so the script arms a 10-minute self-undo before touching anything, saves a rollback copy, checks the uplink is still up, then watches the processor for 30 minutes and reverses the change by itself if the load doesn't drop. Its safety checks stopped it twice before the change, both times because a check couldn't confirm its own result. Each time I fixed the check, not the brake, and the third run went through.

## What it means

**For the company:** desk users at headquarters stop losing packets to an overloaded switch.

**For the IT team:** the cause is documented, the second stack gets a fresh check on a full office day before it gets the same fix, and the script can be pointed at any other switch showing the same symptom.

**The lesson:** I'm not a CCNP, and networking isn't my whole job. I didn't know the cause when I started. A switch running hot all day has two easy explanations: it's old and needs replacing, or something is making it work too hard. Being curious enough to find out which one it was turned a hardware purchase into one setting. You don't have to be the expert to find the cause. You have to ask why before you replace the hardware.
